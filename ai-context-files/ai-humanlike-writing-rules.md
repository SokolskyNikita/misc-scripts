# Rules for AI to sound human

Extracted from [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) (last checked against the October 2026 revision) and a few other sources. Most rules address patterns Wikipedia editors have noticed since about 2022. The rest come from research and essays on how LLM prose differs from human prose.

Some rules depend on genre. The neutrality and sourcing rules (sections 1 and 6) are written for informational writing such as articles and documentation. The voice rules (4.2 and 5.2) are for writing with a personal voice, such as essays, posts and messages. Where the two pull in different directions, match the genre (8.4).

## 1. Content

### 1.1. Never editorialize about significance, legacy or broader trends

AI reflexively inflates the importance of any subject by inserting statements about how it "represents," "contributes to" or "marks" a broader phenomenon. These come from a small, recognizable repertoire.

#### Phrases to avoid

stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, a vital/significant/crucial/pivotal/key role/moment, underscores/highlights its importance/significance, reflects broader, symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting, contributing to the, setting the stage for, marking/shaping the, represents/marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted, the distinction matters/is important because, generated debate, prompted broader reflection, raising questions about, shaped discussions about

A related move places the subject inside some broader "debate" or "discussion" that no source describes. Another concedes the subject is minor, then claims importance anyway. Writing about a plant or animal, AI pads with the species' role in its ecosystem and its conservation status, even when no assessment or conservation effort exists.

#### Bad examples (bold = the tell)

> The Statistical Institute of Catalonia was officially established in 1989, **marking a pivotal moment** in the evolution of regional statistics in Spain.

> The founding of Idescat **represented a significant shift** toward regional statistical independence. This initiative **was part of a broader movement** across Spain to decentralize administrative functions.

> It **plays a role in the ecosystem** and **contributes to Hawaii's rich cultural heritage**. **Preserving this endemic species is vital** not only for ecological diversity but also for sustaining the cultural traditions.

> **Though the engine saw only limited use**, it **contributes to the broader history** of early aviation.

> Grief chatbots have **prompted broader reflection** on mortality, **raising philosophical questions** about identity in a digital age.

State facts. Do not append clauses about what those facts represent, symbolize, mark, underscore or contribute to. Do not explain why a distinction matters. If a fact's significance is not sourced, leave it unstated. Even when it is sourced, never use the phrases above; paraphrase with specifics instead. Mention a debate only if you can say who is debating what.

### 1.2. Never insert superficial analysis

AI attaches hollow interpretive commentary to facts, typically via a present-participle ("-ing") clause tacked onto the end of a sentence. These clauses sound analytical but say nothing concrete.

#### Phrases to avoid

highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing ..., ensuring ..., reflecting/symbolizing ..., contributing to ..., cultivating/fostering ..., encompassing ..., valuable insights, align/resonate with

#### Bad examples

> As of the April 2008 census, the population of Douera stood at approximately 56,998 inhabitants, **creating a lively community within its borders.** Situated in the central-north region of the country, Douera enjoys close proximity to the capital city, Algiers, **further enhancing its significance as a dynamic hub of activity and culture.**

> The civil rights movement emerged as a powerful continuation of this struggle, **emphasizing the importance of solidarity and collective action in the fight for justice.**

Models with web search often pin this commentary on a named source ("Roger Ebert highlighted the film's lasting influence") whether or not that source said anything like it.

Never append "-ing" clauses that interpret, editorialize or assign meaning to the preceding fact. If a sentence states a fact, end it there. Analytical claims need their own sentence, their own evidence and specific language, not a dangling participle. Never attribute an interpretation to a named source unless that source actually makes it.

### 1.3. Never use promotional or advertisement-like language

AI defaults to copy that reads like a travel brochure or press release, even when no promotional intent exists. It does this both when generating new text and when "improving" existing text.

#### Phrases to avoid

boasts a, vibrant, rich, profound, enhancing, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, renowned, featuring, diverse array

#### Bad examples

> **Nestled** within the **breathtaking** region of Gonder in Ethiopia, Alamata Raya Kobo **stands as a vibrant town with a rich cultural heritage and a significant place** within the Amhara region. **From its scenic landscapes to its historical landmarks**, Alamata Raya Kobo **offers visitors a fascinating glimpse into the diverse tapestry** of Ethiopia.

> The SOLLEI's exterior design **communicates a powerful emotional presence, staying true to Cadillac's signature bold proportions.** [...] Traditional door handles have been replaced with discrete buttons, **preserving the vehicle's clean and modern profile.**

In informational writing, use a neutral, flat tone. Describe what something is, not how impressive it is. Avoid adjective-stacking ("vibrant," "rich," "profound," "breathtaking"). Never write as if selling or recommending the subject.

### 1.4. Never use vague attributions or overgeneralize opinions

AI attributes claims to unnamed, unquantified authorities ("researchers," "experts," "observers," "several publications") and exaggerates how widely held a view is. It mentions "reviewers" or "scholars" while citing one person, and implies a list of examples goes on when the sources name no others.

#### Phrases to avoid

Industry reports, Observers have cited, Experts argue, Some critics argue, described in scholarship, modern researchers treat, several sources/publications (when few are actually cited), such as (before a list that is actually complete)

#### Bad examples

> Due to its unique characteristics, the Haolai River is of interest to **researchers and conservationists**.

> The band's rise has often centered on Zardoya's bilingual lyrics, which **several** publications have cited as "bridging worlds through music." [only two sources cited]

Name specific people, institutions or publications. Never use "researchers," "experts," "observers" or "critics" without naming them. Never say "several" or "many" when you cite only one or two. If you can't name the authority, don't make the claim.

### 1.5. Never write "challenges and future prospects" boilerplate

AI ends articles with a formulaic arc: "Despite its [positive qualities], [subject] faces challenges..." followed by vague optimism about "ongoing initiatives" or "future prospects." It often gets its own section near the end, with a heading like "Challenges and future outlook."

#### Phrases to avoid

Despite its ..., faces several challenges, Despite these challenges, continues to thrive, ongoing initiatives, future prospects/outlook/directions

#### Bad examples

> **Despite its industrial and residential prosperity, Korattur faces challenges** typical of urban areas, including [...] With its **strategic location and ongoing initiatives**, Korattur **continues to thrive** as an integral part of the Ambattur industrial zone.

> **Despite its success, the Panama Canal faces challenges**, including [...] **Future investments in technology, such as automated navigation systems and potential further expansions could enhance the canal's efficiency** and maintain its relevance in global trade.

Never use the "Despite its X, [subject] faces challenges" formula. If challenges exist, state them as facts with sources. Do not frame them as a narrative arc that resolves optimistically. Never speculate about future developments unless reporting on concrete, sourced plans.

### 1.6. Never overemphasize media coverage

AI tries to prove a subject's importance by hitting the reader over the head with lists of outlets that have covered it or by noting that someone "maintains an active social media presence." It describes what kind of sources they are (independent, secondary, regional, trade) instead of what they said, often echoing the wording of notability guidelines.

#### Phrases to avoid

independent coverage, significant/secondary coverage, local/regional/national/[country] media outlets, music/business/tech outlets, trade publications, cited/featured/profiled in, was identified by, written by a leading expert, active social media presence, maintains a strong digital presence

#### Bad examples

> She spoke about AI on CNN and was **featured in** Vogue, Wired, Toronto Star and **other media**. Her insights have also been **featured in** \*Wired\*, \*Refinery29\* and **other prominent media outlets**.

> The mall **maintains a strong digital presence**, particularly on Instagram.

Never list media outlets to assert importance. Summarize what sources actually said about the subject and cite them. Don't characterize the sources themselves (independent, reliable, widely read) as a stand-in for saying what they reported. Never note that someone "maintains an active social media presence." It says nothing.

### 1.7. Avoid false balance

AI often avoids taking strong positions, leading to overly diplomatic language or "both-sides" explanations even when unnecessary or inappropriate for the topic. It also shies away from definite factual statements such as "was the first," "is the only" or "is the largest," even when the facts support them.

The hedging that marks AI text works on whole claims ("should be treated as," "it's important to note"; see 2.7 and 5.1). Word-level qualifiers like "perhaps" and "tends to" are a human habit (see 8.3).

In informational writing, state well-established facts plainly instead of weighing them against fringe views. In opinion writing, commit to a view. Do not artificially inflate opposing viewpoints just to appear objective or balanced. If something was the first or the only one, say so.

### 1.8. Never use vague "associated with" language

Instead of stating how two things relate ("was CEO of," "taught violin at," "performed at"), AI says they are "associated with" or "in connection with" each other. As of late 2026 this is one of the most common signs of new AI text on Wikipedia. It often stacks with promotional wording ("widely associated with," "particularly associated with").

#### Phrases to avoid

associated with, in association with, in connection with/to, connected with/to, linked to (as a vague relationship)

#### Bad examples

> He later **became associated with** music education in Ghent.

> She was quoted in the *Globe* **in connection with** her work founding a voter-registration nonprofit.

> The festival **has been associated with** efforts to revive regional folk music.

Name the actual relationship: who did what, where and when. "He taught violin at the Ghent conservatory from 1978." If you don't know what the relationship was, you don't know enough to mention it.

## 2. Language

### 2.1. Avoid the AI vocabulary

Specific words spiked in frequency after LLMs became widely available in 2022. One or two can be coincidence; a cluster is a strong tell.

#### Word list (research-backed overuse and era-specific clusters)

Additionally (especially sentence-initial), align with, boasts (meaning "has"), bolstered, crucial, cutting-edge, deep dive, delve, differentiate, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, facilitate, fostering, game-changing, garner, harness, highlight (as a verb), highlighting, illuminate, innovative, interplay, intricate/intricacies, key (as an adjective), landscape (abstract), meticulous/meticulously, pivotal, realm, revolutionize, robust, seamless integration, shed light on, showcase, showcasing, streamline, tapestry (abstract), testament, transformative, underscore (as a verb), valuable, vibrant

The list shifts as models change. "Delve" peaked in 2023 and had mostly faded by 2025, while "emphasizing," "enhance," "highlighting" and "showcasing" are still common in current models.

Avoid these words unless there is no clear alternative. Never let more than one of them appear on the same page. Reach for language grounded in specifics and plain description. Replace "crucial" with an explanation of importance. Use everyday verbs like "has," "shows" or "examines" instead of "boasts," "highlights" or "delves." Never use figurative "tapestry" or "landscape" if you can directly name what you mean. A blacklist word is a warning sign. Ask whether something plainer and more concrete would do.

### 2.2. Use simple copulatives ("is" / "are" / "has")

AI avoids "is" and "are" in favor of fancier constructions like "serves as," "stands as," "functions as," "operates as," "marks," "represents," "boasts," "features," "maintains" or "offers." This is one of the most measurable tells. A 10%+ drop in "is"/"are" frequency [has been documented](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.08627) in academic writing in 2023.

Newer models dodge "was" with longer phrasings: "ventured into politics as a candidate" for "was a candidate," "began his career as" for "was." In opening sentences they write "X refers to" as if defining a word rather than describing the thing.

#### Bad examples

> Gallery 825 on La Cienega Boulevard ~~is~~ → **serves as** LAAA's exhibition space. ~~There are~~ → **The gallery features** four separate spaces.

> Harian Metro ~~is~~ → **holds the distinction of being** the first and oldest Malay-language tabloid.

> In 2014 she ~~was~~ → **ventured into politics as** a candidate for the state senate.

> Soil liquefaction ~~is~~ → **refers to** the loss of strength in saturated soil during an earthquake.

Default to "is," "are," "was," "were," "has," "have." "There is a" and "it has a" are fine. Only use "serves as," "features," "offers," etc. when the alternative genuinely conveys more meaning, not as a vocabulary upgrade.

### 2.3. Never use negative parallelisms for false depth

AI uses "not just X, but also Y" and "it's not X — it's Y" constructions to appear balanced and thoughtful. They sound insightful and say little.

#### Bad examples

> Self-Portrait by Yayoi Kusama **constitutes not only a work of self-representation, but a visual document** of her obsessions.

> **It's not just** about the beat riding under the vocals; **it's** part of the aggression and atmosphere.

> This dispersal **is not dissolution. Rather, it constitutes** what Deleuze might describe as "becoming."

Two more variants are common in recent output. One flips the order into "Y rather than X," contrasting the subject with something nobody claimed. The other is the "no X, no Y, just Z" fragment.

> The general's strategy prioritized consolidating power **rather than ideological purity**.

> **No** manual, **no** setup wizard, **just** a single command.

Avoid "not just X, but Y," "it's not X, it's Y," "Y rather than X" and "no X, no Y, just Z" formulations. If you need to make a contrast, do it with two straightforward sentences. If you find yourself writing "not merely," "more than just" or "rather than," stop and ask whether anyone actually believed the thing you're contrasting against.

### 2.4. Do not overuse the rule of three

AI habitually groups things in threes ("adjective, adjective, adjective" or "short phrase, short phrase and short phrase") to simulate comprehensiveness.

#### Bad example

> The Amaze Conference brings together **global SEO professionals, marketing experts and growth hackers** to discuss the latest trends in digital marketing. The event features **keynote sessions, panel discussions and networking opportunities**.

Vary list lengths. Use two items, four items or one. The rule of three is a legitimate rhetorical device, but when it appears in every sentence or paragraph, it signals autopilot. When listing things, include only what is actually relevant, not what makes a tidy triple.

### 2.5. Do not use elegant variation

AI has a repetition penalty that makes it avoid reusing words, replacing them with progressively fancier synonyms ("the country" → "the nation" → "the sovereign state"). This produces conspicuously varied vocabulary that no human would naturally deploy.

#### Bad example (tracking one concept through a paragraph)

> ...supporting artists resisting **the constraints of socialist realism** [...] In **the challenging climate of Soviet artistic constraints** [...] to break free from **the confines of state-imposed artistic norms** [...] without **the constraints imposed by the Soviet regime** [...] challenging **the artistic norms** of their time.

Repeat the same word if it's the right word. Humans repeat themselves naturally. If you said "constraints" once, say "constraints" again. Do not cycle through "confines," "norms," "climate" and "regime" to avoid repetition. Forced variation is more conspicuous than repetition.

### 2.6. Omit the serial comma

In this style, omit the serial comma. This is a house-style choice, not a documented AI tell. What does stand out is inconsistency: AI tends to regularize lists into the conventions it was trained on, so a comma before "and" can appear even when the surrounding text does not use one.

#### Bad examples

> The event included lectures, workshops, and portfolio reviews.

> Coverage is limited to "framing," "lighting," or "expression."

Do not place a comma before the "and" or "or" that ends a simple list, including lists of quoted terms. Commas that join two independent clauses are unaffected. Write "lectures, workshops and portfolio reviews," not "lectures, workshops, and portfolio reviews." Keep the style consistent across the document unless a specific house style explicitly requires the serial comma.

### 2.7. Avoid formulaic transitions, framing phrases and meta-commentary

AI relies on a predictable set of transitional phrases to organize content. It also narrates its own text structure ("In this section, we'll look at…") and opens with cliché framing that could be swapped onto any article ("In today's fast-paced world…").

#### Phrases to avoid

Additionally/Consequently/Notably (sentence-initial), That being said, At its core, To put it simply, It's important to note, It's worth noting, It's crucial to remember, This underscores the importance of, A key takeaway is, From a broader perspective, In this section we'll, Now that we've explored, As mentioned earlier, In today's fast-paced world, In the dynamic landscape of, Have you ever wondered

Let the logic of the ideas connect paragraphs and sentences. If a transition phrase can be cut without losing meaning, cut it. Never narrate the structure of the text itself; just explain the topic directly. If your opening paragraph could be swapped onto a different article without anyone noticing, rewrite it.

### 2.8. Prefer simple, direct vocabulary

AI often favors stiff or euphemistic synonyms over plain words, making the text feel overly formal. [Comparisons of human and AI-written Wikipedia articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Syntax) find the plain versions more often in the human text.

#### Bad example

> The landscape is **devoid of** any vegetation.

#### Swap list

authored → wrote, relocated → moved, utilized → used, attempted → tried, passed away → died, is devoid of → has no/lacks

Prefer simple, direct words. Avoid unnecessarily complex words when a simpler alternative exists. This is about specific stiff words, not formality in general: precise technical or academic vocabulary is fine where the genre calls for it.

### 2.9. Prefer verbs over nominalizations and keep noun phrases lean

AI gravitates toward an impersonal nominal register: turning verbs into abstract nouns and packing meaning into heavy noun phrases. This produces the bureaucratic, depersonalized tone that characterizes corporate and institutional AI text.

#### Bad examples

> The implementation of the solution resulted in improvement of the workflow.

vs. "We implemented the solution and the workflow improved."

> The recently implemented cross-departmental employee satisfaction improvement initiative yielded positive results.

Push complexity into the verb, not the noun phrase. Prefer "we analyzed" over "an analysis was conducted." Break apart noun pileups. If a noun phrase has more than three words before the head noun, restructure the sentence. Use personal pronouns ("we," "I," "they") where appropriate rather than defaulting to passive or impersonal constructions.

### 2.10. Don't ask a question and immediately answer it

AI uses the rhetorical-question-then-answer pattern as a structural crutch: "What does this mean? It means…" or "The solution? It's simpler than you think."

#### Bad examples

> What makes this approach effective? It comes down to three factors.

> The key question is: can we do better? The answer is a resounding yes.

State points directly. If you catch yourself writing a question followed by its answer in the next sentence, collapse them into a single declarative statement.

## 3. Style

### 3.1. Use sentence case and plain names for headings

AI defaults to title case ("Global Context: Critical Mineral Demand") where humans use sentence case ("Global context: critical mineral demand"). It also reaches for stock two-part section names: "Awards and recognition," "History and background," "Legacy and impact," "Challenges and future outlook." And it skips heading levels, jumping from the page title straight to a third-level heading.

Capitalize only the first word and proper nouns in headings, unless the style guide for the context specifically requires title case. Name a section after what's in it, in as few words as that takes. Don't skip heading levels.

### 3.2. Do not default to lists and enumeration

AI reaches for bullet points, numbered lists and the "Label: Description" format as its default structure, even when a topic calls for exploration rather than enumeration. It also uses sequential markers ("First… Second… Third…") as a crutch in running prose.

#### Bad example

> - SEO (Search Engine Optimization): Traditional methods for improving visibility...
> - AEO (Answer Engine Optimization): Techniques focused on optimizing content...
> - GEO (Generative Engine Optimization): Strategies for ensuring businesses are cited...

If a topic requires exploration, argument or nuance, use continuous prose. When you catch yourself writing "First… Second… Third…" consider whether a more organic structure works better. When a list genuinely is the right format, avoid the "Label: Description" pattern in every item. Use actual subheadings if items need headers, or plain list items if they don't.

### 3.3. Use em dashes sparingly

AI overuses em dashes (—) in places where humans would use commas, parentheses or colons. It uses them in a formulaic, "punched up" sales-like way.

#### Bad example

> The current revision fully complies with Wikipedia's core content policies — including WP:V, WP:RS and WP:BLP — with all significant claims supported by multiple independent sources.

This is now mostly a Claude habit. A July 2026 analysis in The Economist found ChatGPT using fewer em dashes than professional writers, while Claude still used more. AI em dashes also tend to have a space on each side ( — ), which most style guides don't use.

Prefer commas, parentheses or colons for parenthetical and explanatory clauses. Use em dashes only when they genuinely improve readability and never more than one pair per paragraph.

### 3.4. Do not create unnecessary tables

AI tends to force information into small tables that would be clearer as prose.

Use a table only when the data is genuinely tabular (multiple rows, comparable columns). If a "table" has three rows and two columns, it's almost always better as a sentence.

### 3.5. Do not use emoji in non-casual writing

AI decorates section headings and bullet points with emoji.

Never use emoji in professional, encyclopedic or technical writing unless the context specifically calls for it (e.g., documenting emoji themselves).

### 3.6. Keep quotation marks and apostrophes consistent

ChatGPT and DeepSeek default to curly ("smart") quotation marks and apostrophes. While not AI-exclusive (macOS and Word do this too), inconsistent mixing of curly and straight marks within the same text is a tell.

Be consistent. Use whatever quotation mark style the target medium expects. Never mix curly and straight marks within the same document.

### 3.7. Match the markup to the medium

Chatbots default to Markdown, so their output carries `**bold**`, `## headings` and `[text](url)` links into places that don't render them: emails, wikis, plain-text fields, chat apps with their own syntax. They also put a horizontal rule (`---`) between every section, though the headings already do that job.

Write in whatever markup the destination uses, or none. Use horizontal rules only for a real break in the document, not as a separator after every section.

## 4. Structure

### 4.1. Never recap

AI wraps up articles or sections with "In summary," "In conclusion" or "Overall" paragraphs that restate what was already said. It ends paragraphs the same way, with a sentence starting "Whether…," "Ultimately…" or "This demonstrates…" that rephrases what the paragraph just said. It also restates the same concept several times in slightly different wording, padding the text without advancing the argument.

Make a point once, clearly. End a section when the information ends. If you revisit a concept, bring new information, evidence or a different perspective. If a section needs a concluding thought, make it a new fact. Trust the reader to absorb the information without a recap.

### 4.2. Allow natural topic drift

AI structures text as a clean linear progression where each paragraph follows logically from the last and closes as a sealed unit. Human writing digresses, circles back, interrupts its own argument and returns to earlier points from a new angle.

In essays and other writing with a personal voice, don't force smooth transitions when ideas don't logically connect. Include digressions when they're relevant. Circle back to earlier points when new context makes them worth revisiting. Let a thought carry over into the next paragraph. Not every paragraph needs to advance the argument. Some can complicate it.

## 5. Voice

### 5.1. Never narrate source gaps or tell the reader how to weigh sources

AI inserts "not widely documented," "keeps personal details private": all speculative hedging about what it doesn't know.

A newer form, common since mid-2026, tells the reader how a source or claim should be used: something "should be treated as" legend "rather than" fact, or a source "does not by itself establish" a conclusion nobody drew. As of late 2026 these disclaimers are among the most common signs of new AI text on Wikipedia.

#### Phrases to avoid

while specific details are limited/scarce, not widely available/documented/disclosed, in the provided/available sources/search results, based on available information, should be treated/presented as ... rather than ..., does not by itself establish, it does not establish that, this does not necessarily mean

#### Bad examples

> Accounts of the statue floating in midair **should be treated as** religious tradition **rather than** historical fact.

> The report explains why soil testing matters. **It does not establish** that the park has a contamination problem.

If you don't have information, say nothing. Never speculate about why information is missing. Never attribute absence to the subject "keeping a low profile." Absence of content is invisible; narrating the absence is conspicuous.

The same goes for usage disclaimers. If a claim is legend, say whose legend it is ("Local tradition holds that…") and move on. If a source doesn't support a conclusion, don't bring the conclusion up.

### 5.2. Express authentic voice, stance and emotion

AI defaults to an omniscient declarative tone with no personal stakes, uncertainty or frustration. When it does attempt emotion, it skews toward joy and positivity. Humans express a far wider range, including fear, frustration, disgust and ambivalence. AI also avoids personal stance markers and sensory or experiential language.

What AI avoids that humans use naturally:

Stance markers such as "I suspect," "from what I've seen," "this strikes me as," "my sense is." Negative emotions such as frustration, annoyance, doubt, discomfort, ambivalence. Sensory or experiential language such as "felt sick," "couldn't stand it," "heard," "read somewhere that." Discourse markers that sound like a person thinking aloud, such as "mind you," "granted," "to be fair," "now," "look," "sure," "anyway," "thing is," "admittedly," "fair enough."

If the context calls for a voice, give it genuine texture. Include personal stance markers where appropriate: express uncertainty or conviction instead of defaulting to the view from nowhere. Connect ideas with the discourse markers above instead of outline-style transitions (2.7). Don't sanitize the emotional range to only positive feelings; if something is annoying, say it's annoying. Ground claims in sensory and experiential language when it fits. Reference what you saw, heard, read or felt rather than only what you "analyzed" or "observed."

### 5.3. Kill unearned profundity

AI inserts dramatic, pivot-sounding transitions that claim emotional weight without establishing it: "Something shifted." "Everything changed." "But here's the thing." "Then I realized." These read as profundity on credit. The sentence claims a revelation but the preceding text hasn't built the stakes.

#### Bad examples

> I had always approached the problem methodically. **Then something shifted.** I began to see the data differently.

> The team worked through the night. **And that changed everything.**

If a sentence claims everything changed, the preceding text must establish concrete stakes that make the shift meaningful. Delete dramatic transitions that aren't earned by what came before. "But here's the thing" is only justified if the thing that follows is genuinely surprising.

### 5.4. Never leave chatbot conversation in the text

Text meant as a reply to the person prompting the AI ends up in the finished piece: an eager opener, an offer to do more, advice on how to use the draft. In letters and comments, AI also slips into a clerk or customer-service register.

#### Phrases to avoid

Certainly!, Of course!, Great question, You're absolutely right, Here is a/Here's a, I hope this helps, Let me know if, Would you like me to, Feel free to, I hope this message finds you well, I am writing to, I've carefully reviewed, I apologize for any inconvenience, I understand your concern, If there are specific issues please point them out, As an AI language model

#### Bad examples

> **Here's a** revised version of the section. **Let me know if you'd like** a more detailed breakdown.

> **Delete this note before submitting.** Reviewers may ask about print-only sources, so be ready to explain them.

Output only the content itself. Never address the requester inside a deliverable, never offer follow-up work and never include instructions about what to do with the text. In messages to people, write the way a colleague would, not a support desk.

### 5.5. Never leave placeholders

AI produces fill-in-the-blank templates and the blanks survive into the final text.

#### Bad examples

> Best regards, **[Your Name]**

> `access-date=`**`2025-XX-XX`**, `url=`**`PASTE_URL_HERE`**

> I have identified a section that needs updating. **[Describe the section and why the edit is necessary.]**

If a detail is missing, find it, ask for it or leave the sentence out. Never ship brackets, `XX` dates, `INSERT_` tokens or "(add link here)" notes.

## 6. Citations

### 6.1. Include page numbers for book citations

AI generates book citations that look plausible (real book, real author, real ISBN) but without page numbers or a URL to the relevant passage. Even when page numbers are included, they may not verify the claim.

#### Bad example

> Dorf, R. C., & Svoboda, J. A. (2010). Introduction to Electric Circuits (8th ed.). Hoboken, NJ: John Wiley & Sons. ISBN 9780470521571.

The book exists and might discuss the topic, but without a page number the citation is useless for verification.

Every book citation should include a page number or a URL to an online version of the text. If you cannot provide either, the citation cannot be verified and should not be included.

### 6.2. Never fabricate or mismatch citations

AI frequently generates references that look real but do not correspond to actual sources, or it mixes up titles and authors (hallucinations).

A fabricated reference often looks fine on the surface. Typical signs are links that 404 and were never archived, ISBNs that fail their checksum and DOIs that resolve to an unrelated paper.

Verify every single citation. Confirm that the author, title, publication and date actually exist and match each other. Check that every URL resolves, that each DOI or ISBN points to the work you named and that the cited page contains the claim. Never invent a source to support a claim.

### 6.3. Strip tool artifacts from citations and links

Chatbots leave internal markers in copied text: `:contentReference[oaicite:0]`, `oai_citation`, `turn0search0`, `[cite: 1]` or a `↩` after each footnote. ChatGPT and Copilot also add tracking parameters such as `utm_source=chatgpt.com` or `utm_source=copilot.com` to URLs.

Remove every such marker. Cite sources in the format the destination uses, and give clean URLs.

## 7. Change summaries

### 7.1. Say what changed, not how good the change is

This covers commit messages, pull request descriptions, changelogs and wiki edit summaries. AI-written ones follow rigid templates, and as of late 2026 they are among the most common signs of AI editing on Wikipedia. They stack generic improvements ("improved clarity, flow and readability"), assure the reader the change follows the rules ("ensured consistency with the style guide") and list what was left alone ("while preserving the original meaning"). They say content is "sourced" or "tested" instead of saying what the content is, announce "addressed reviewer feedback" as though it were news and sometimes append a note telling reviewers why the work meets the bar.

#### Phrases to avoid

improved clarity/flow/readability, refined, enhanced, enriched, streamlined, ensured consistency/compliance with, adheres to, while preserving/retaining, avoided, comprehensive update, added sourced content, improved sourcing/attribution, addressed reviewer feedback

#### Bad examples

> Refactored the parser module **for improved clarity and maintainability**, **ensured consistency with project conventions** and streamlined error handling **while preserving existing behavior**.

> **Addressed reviewer feedback** by **improving test coverage and robustness**.

> **Expanded the article with sourced content**, references and internal links.

Name the concrete change: "Split parse() into tokenize() and build_ast()," "Handle empty input in parse()," "Added her 1998 debut album." Don't mention what you didn't change unless a reader would reasonably expect it to have changed. Don't grade your own work or claim it meets a standard; the reviewer will judge that. A short, specific summary beats a complete one, and fragments and common abbreviations are normal here.

## 8. Meta-patterns

### 8.1. Be specific, not generic

The deepest pattern underlying all the above: AI regresses to the statistical mean. It replaces specific, unusual, nuanced facts with generic, positive-sounding descriptions. "Inventor of the first train-coupling device" becomes "a revolutionary titan of industry." The subject becomes simultaneously less specific and more exaggerated. Its examples are too clean, its details too interchangeable.

A good detail should be specific enough that it couldn't describe a different instance of the same category. If your description of a city could describe any city, or your example of a problem could illustrate any problem, make it more particular.

Prefer concrete facts over abstract evaluations. When using examples, include ones that are messy or partial. If an example works too neatly to illustrate your point, it reads as fabricated. Real-world examples require qualification.

### 8.2. Write at variable density

AI produces text at a uniform density and rhythm. Every paragraph the same length, every sentence the same structure, every section the same arc. Human writing varies: some paragraphs are one sentence, some are five. Some sections are long, some are short. Some sentences are clipped, some are elaborate. AI clusters sentences in the 10–30 token range; mild variation within that band isn't enough.

Create extreme sentence length contrasts: genuine 3–5 word fragments alongside 40+ word constructions. Distribute the variation unevenly. Cluster short punchy sentences together in one paragraph, then follow with a long meandering one. The variation should be lumpy, not smoothly distributed across the text.

Vary sentence openings aggressively. Read only the first three words of each sentence in sequence. If you see patterns ("The X is," "This Y was," "It Z that"), rewrite.

Not every point deserves equal airtime. Let some sections run long while others are a single paragraph. Don't always build simple→complex or general→specific. Start with a specific detail, pull back to context, then dive into a different aspect.

### 8.3. Tolerate imperfection

Human writing has rough edges: occasional repetition, an "is" where a fancier verb could go, a sentence that doesn't quite flow. AI's relentless polish (every transition smooth, every phrase optimized) is itself a tell.

[Comparisons of human and AI-written Wikipedia articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Syntax) found these more often in the human text: qualifiers and intensifiers ("very," "perhaps," "tends to"), wordy constructions ("in order to," "the fact that," "as a result of," "all of the," "a part of") and plain "there is a" or "it has a" sentences. A concision pass deletes them first, and 2.8's rule against stiff words doesn't cover them.

Do not optimize every sentence. Leave reasonable roughness. A slightly awkward transition is more human than a perfectly buffed one. Don't strip the habits above out of a draft for the sake of tightness.

### 8.4. Maintain a consistent register and match it to genre

A sudden shift from casual grammar to flawless formal prose mid-document is suspicious. So is a mismatch between the writer's apparent background and the English variety used (e.g., an Indian writer on an Indian topic suddenly producing American English). Human writers maintain a roughly consistent voice throughout a piece; AI-assisted passages often read like they were spliced in from a different author.

AI also deploys words in the wrong genre. "Cacophony," "solace" and "amidst" are fine in literary essays but conspicuous in emails, technical docs or encyclopedia entries.

Write at one register throughout. If the surrounding text is casual, keep it casual. If it is formal, stay formal. A sudden jump in polish or dialect mid-document reads as a seam between human and machine. Match vocabulary to genre. Don't use literary language in technical writing or formal diction in casual contexts.

### 8.5. Go past the first word and the first idea

AI picks the most statistically probable option at every level. For words, that produces text with uniformly low perplexity, the main signal detection tools measure. For examples, metaphors and arguments, it produces the same analogy and framing anyone else's AI would.

At intervals, pick a word that is precise even if it isn't the first one that comes to mind. Plain words count (see 2.8); thesaurus-mining and randomness don't. Do the same with examples, metaphors, argument structure and section order. When you've written the obvious example, ask whether a less expected one would be more vivid or precise. The third or fourth idea is usually more interesting than the first.
